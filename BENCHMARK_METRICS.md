# SAXS-TT Benchmark Metrics

This document explains every metric computed in `notebooks/saxs_naf_benchmark.ipynb`
and implemented in `smartt/saxs_naf/metrics.py`: what it measures, why we compute it
that way, and what to expect from a reconstruction that is actually working.

---

## 0. Representation: SH coefficients

Every reconstruction — regardless of method (NAF, mumott-SH, mumott-GK, FBP) — is stored
as a `(X, Y, Z, C)` float32 array of real spherical harmonic (SH) coefficients, where
`C = (ell_max/2 + 1)^2` with only even-ℓ terms retained (SAXS patterns have inversion
symmetry, so odd-ℓ terms are exactly zero).  For `ell_max=8` this gives `C=45`.

At each voxel `(x, y, z)` the SH coefficient vector encodes the full angular distribution
of SAXS intensity in reciprocal space — equivalently, the local reciprocal-space map (RSM).
`c[x,y,z,0]` (the ℓ=0, m=0 coefficient, written `c00`) is proportional to the mean
scattering intensity (isotropic signal); the higher-ℓ coefficients encode the anisotropy.

---

## 1. Ground truth

### How it is constructed

The ground truth `coeffs_gt` is a mumott reconstruction run on the **combined DataContainer**:
the union of all available measurement mounts (e.g. the original scan plus a remounted scan
at a different tilt).  This combined dataset has near-full angular coverage of the sample,
so it does not suffer from a significant missing wedge.

The method is configurable (`GT_METHOD = 'sh'` or `'gk'`).  In both cases the output is
converted to `(X,Y,Z,C)` SH coefficients so all methods speak the same language.

### Why this is a valid reference

With full angular coverage the reconstruction problem is well-posed: every voxel is
illuminated from enough angles that the SAXS intensity distribution is determined (up to
noise and regularization bias).  The partial-mount reconstructions are evaluated by asking
how closely they recover this reference.

### What makes a good GT

The GT reconstruction should converge well (check LBFGS residual).  20 iterations with
Laplacian weight 0.1 is typically sufficient — the Laplacian regularizer penalizes spatial
roughness and prevents overfitting to noise.

---

## 2. Holdout split

Before computing any metric that involves the training data, 10% of projections are
withheld at random (`split_holdout`, seeded for reproducibility).  The training methods
only see the remaining 90%.  This gives an unbiased consistency check (§8) that does not
require a ground-truth reconstruction at all.

When `HOLDOUT_FRAC=0.0` (the current default) holdout NRMSE is not meaningful — it is
computed on an empty held-out set and should be ignored.

---

## 3. Spatial mask

All voxel-level metrics (RSM correlation, RA MAE, orientation error, PSNR, SSIM, NRMSE)
are evaluated only inside a **tissue mask** obtained by Otsu thresholding on the GT `c00`
volume.  This excludes air and embedding medium voxels where the signal-to-noise is near
zero and the RSM is dominated by noise.

If `mask=None` the Otsu threshold is applied automatically.  Pass `mask=False` to disable
masking (useful for synthetic phantoms that fill the full volume).

---

## 4. Evaluation directions — Fibonacci hemisphere

The SH expansion is evaluated at `K=30` unit vectors generated by
`fibonacci_hemisphere(K, half_space='y')`.  These are quasi-uniformly distributed on the
upper hemisphere (the half-space convention matches the SAXS scattering geometry where the
beam propagates along y and q-vectors span the half-sphere orthogonal to it).

Using a fixed, reproducible set of directions is essential: it makes the per-direction
missing-arc bucketing (§5) meaningful and ensures that the RSM correlation (§5) is not
biased toward directions that happen to be well-sampled by a specific method.

---

## 5. RSM Pearson correlation

### Scalar: `rsm_corr_mean`
### Map: `rsm_corr_map`  (X, Y, Z)
### Per-arc-bucket: `rsm_corr_by_arc`

**What it measures.** For each voxel we have two vectors of length K: the GT RSM
intensities at the K evaluation directions, and the predicted RSM intensities.  We compute
the Pearson correlation coefficient between those two vectors.  The result is a scalar in
`[-1, 1]` per voxel — a map of how well the full angular shape of the scattering pattern
is recovered everywhere in the sample.

Formally:

```
I_pred[k,x,y,z] = Σ_c  B[k,c] · coeffs[x,y,z,c]
```

where `B[k,c]` is the real SH basis matrix evaluated at direction k.  Then:

```
rsm_corr[x,y,z] = Pearson_r( I_gt[:, x, y, z],  I_pred[:, x, y, z] )
```

`rsm_corr_mean` is the average of this map over masked voxels.

**Why Pearson r, not MSE.** Pearson r measures the *shape* of the RSM regardless of
overall scale.  Two reconstructions with completely different absolute intensities (due to,
say, different normalization conventions) can still have Pearson r=1 if their angular
patterns are identical.  This is the physically relevant quantity: in SAXS-TT the
direction-dependent intensity profile tells us about fiber orientation and anisotropy, not
the absolute cross-section.

**Bucketing by missing-arc severity.** The `rsm_corr_by_arc` dict splits the K directions
into three groups based on how large the angular gap in coverage is for that direction:

| Bucket         | Arc range | Interpretation |
|----------------|-----------|----------------|
| `low (<60°)`   | < 60°     | Well-sampled directions — least affected by missing wedge |
| `medium (60-120°)` | 60–120° | Partially missing — moderate difficulty |
| `high (>120°)` | > 120°    | Severely missing — reconstruction must extrapolate |

The missing arc for a direction k is calculated analytically from the goniometer tilt angle
(`ALPHA_DEG`) and the direction vector.  A method that degrades gracefully will show only a
moderate drop from low to high buckets; a method that overfits to the available projections
will show a sharp cliff at the high-missing-arc bucket.

**Expected values from a working method.**

- `rsm_corr_mean ≥ 0.85` is strong.  Below 0.7 indicates that the angular pattern is
  poorly recovered — likely a symptom of severe missing-wedge artefacts or over-smoothing.
- The bucket scores should degrade monotonically: `low > medium > high`.
- NAF should score higher than FBP on the high-arc bucket because the neural field can
  extrapolate using the implicit spatial prior, while FBP simply back-projects what it sees.

## 6. Relative anisotropy (RA)

### Scalar: `ra_mae`
### Map: `ra_map`  (X, Y, Z)

**What it measures.** Relative anisotropy is defined per voxel as the coefficient of
variation of the RSM intensities over the K directions:

```
RA[x,y,z] = std_k( I[k,x,y,z] ) / mean_k( I[k,x,y,z] )
```

It is a dimensionless number in [0, ∞): zero for a perfectly isotropic voxel, larger for
voxels with a strong preferred orientation.  `ra_mae` is the mean absolute error of the
predicted RA against the GT RA, over the mask.

**Why it matters.** RA is the primary scalar descriptor of local fiber organization.  It
is proportional to the degree of crystallographic or fibrillar order in the illuminated
volume element.  A reconstruction that preserves `rsm_corr_mean` but has high `ra_mae`
may be recovering the orientation correctly but compressing or exaggerating the contrast
between ordered and disordered regions — a common artefact of over-regularized methods.

**Expected values.**  `ra_mae ≈ 0.01–0.05` is good for biological tissues.  Values above
0.1 suggest that the anisotropy contrast is seriously distorted.

---

## 7. Fiber symmetry

### Scalar: `fiber_symmetry_mae`
### Map: `fiber_symmetry_map`  (X, Y, Z)

**What it measures.** A SAXS pattern from a fibrillar material has approximate cylindrical
(fiber) symmetry around its principal axis.  `mumott`'s
`find_approximate_symmetry_axis` finds the axis `(θ, φ)` that maximizes the fraction of
the SH power concentrated in the zonal harmonics (m=0 terms relative to that axis).

The fiber symmetry factor per voxel is:

```
fiber_sym[x,y,z] = Σ_ℓ  |zonal_coeff[ℓ,0]|² / Σ_ℓ≥2  Σ_m  |coeff[ℓ,m]|²
```

(excluding c00 from both numerator and denominator).  It ranges in [0, 1]: 1 for a
perfectly cylindrically symmetric pattern, 0 for a pattern with all power in non-zonal
modes.  `fiber_symmetry_mae` is the MAE of this map against the GT.

**Why it matters.** Fiber symmetry is a structural quality indicator.  In real bone or
tendon, the local RSM should be close to cylindrically symmetric around the collagen fiber
direction.  A reconstruction that breaks this symmetry (e.g. by introducing azimuthal
ripple artefacts from the missing wedge) will have low fiber_sym even if its RSM
correlation is decent.  Tracking fiber symmetry helps distinguish missing-wedge streaks
from true structural asymmetry.

**Expected values.** Real collagen-mineralized tissues typically have
`fiber_sym ∈ [0.6, 0.9]` in ordered regions.  `fiber_symmetry_mae < 0.05` is good.

---

## 8. Orientation angular error

### Scalar: `orientation_error_mean`
### Map: `orientation_error_map`  (X, Y, Z)

**What it measures.** The principal fiber axis from `find_approximate_symmetry_axis` gives
a unit vector `(θ, φ)` per voxel.  The angular error is:

```
err[x,y,z] = arccos( |pred_axis · gt_axis| )   [degrees]
```

The absolute value of the dot product handles the axis ambiguity (a fiber axis has no
intrinsic head/tail direction).  The scalar `orientation_error_mean` is the RA-weighted
average: voxels with high anisotropy receive more weight because orientation is only
physically meaningful where there is a well-defined anisotropy signal.

**Why it matters.** Orientation is the primary output of a SAXS-TT experiment.  A map of
fiber directions across a section is what allows biological structure–function questions to
be answered.  Getting the orientation wrong by even 10° can change the interpretation of
tissue mechanics models.  This metric is the most direct test of whether the reconstruction
is scientifically useful.

**Why RA-weighting.** In nearly isotropic voxels (RA ≈ 0) the orientation estimate is
noise-dominated and has no physical meaning — the RSM is close to a sphere and any axis
fits equally well.  Weighting by RA concentrates the error measure where orientation is
actually defined.

**Expected values.** Below 5° is excellent.  Below 15° is acceptable.  Above 30° typically
means the reconstruction is confused about fiber orientation in a significant fraction of
the tissue, which would undermine any downstream structural analysis.  A method that
recovers RSM correlation well but has high orientation error is likely over-smoothing in
the angular domain (blurring the orientation signal).

## 9. PSNR, SSIM, NRMSE on c00

### Scalars: `psnr`, `ssim`, `nrmse`

**What they measure.** These are standard image quality metrics applied only to the
isotropic channel `c00 = coeffs[..., 0]`, which is proportional to the spatially resolved
mean SAXS intensity (analogous to a conventional CT image).  They are computed over masked
voxels.

| Metric | Formula | Unit | Range |
|--------|---------|------|-------|
| NRMSE  | `√MSE / std(GT)` | — | [0, ∞), lower is better |
| PSNR   | `10 log₁₀(range² / MSE)` | dB | higher is better |
| SSIM   | structural similarity index | — | [−1, 1], higher is better |

**Why only c00.** The full SH vector has 45 dimensions per voxel; standard image metrics
are designed for scalar fields.  c00 is the most physically interpretable scalar summary
(total scattering cross-section density) and is comparable across methods without
coordinate conventions.

**Why these matter.** Even if RSM correlation is high, a method could still shift the
absolute intensity scale (bad PSNR/NRMSE) or destroy local spatial contrast (bad SSIM).
SSIM in particular penalizes both intensity shifts and loss of local texture, making it
sensitive to over-smoothing by the Laplacian regularizer.

**Why they are insufficient alone.** A method that maps every voxel to the GT c00 but
scrambles the higher-ℓ coefficients would score perfectly on these metrics while failing
completely on orientation recovery.  These metrics are therefore necessary but not
sufficient — they should always be read alongside RSM correlation and orientation error.

**Expected values.** For reasonably regularized methods on bone SAXS-TT data:
- `nrmse < 0.15` is good
- `psnr > 25 dB` is good
- `ssim > 0.80` is good

Aggressive regularization (high Laplacian weight) tends to increase SSIM slightly
(smoother is more "structured") while inflating NRMSE in high-intensity regions.

## 10. Held-out projection NRMSE

### Scalar: `holdout_nrmse`

**What it measures.** This is the only metric that requires no ground truth at all.
After reconstruction, the SH coefficient volume is re-projected through the forward model
onto the held-out projections (the 10% of measurements withheld from training).  The
NRMSE between these synthetic measurements and the actual detector frames is:

```
holdout_nrmse = √MSE(pred_measurements, actual_measurements) / std(actual_measurements)
```

where the MSE is computed over all detector pixels and held-out projections where the
measurement weight is nonzero.

**Why it matters.** This measures consistency with raw data the method has never seen.
A reconstruction that overfits to its training projections (memorizing noise) will
generalize poorly and show high holdout NRMSE even if it looks visually similar to the GT.
Conversely, a reconstruction that is too strongly regularized will also score poorly
because it cannot explain the fine-scale intensity variations in the held-out frames.

This metric is especially important when no combined dataset is available to construct a
ground truth, making it the primary diagnostic for fully self-supervised settings.

**Expected values.**  `holdout_nrmse < 0.10` indicates good generalization.  Values above
0.20 suggest either overfitting to training noise or excessive smoothing.  The holdout
NRMSE for the training projections (not reported, but derivable) should be lower than for
the held-out set; if they are equal, the reconstruction is effectively a trivial constant.

**Caveat: holdout fraction must be > 0.**  If `HOLDOUT_FRAC=0.0` (all projections used for
training), the held-out set is empty and this metric is undefined.  The notebook silently
returns a near-zero value in that case — ignore it.

## 11. Summary: what to look for

| Metric | Good | Marginal | Bad | Primary failure mode when bad |
|--------|------|----------|-----|-------------------------------|
| `rsm_corr_mean` | ≥ 0.85 | 0.70–0.85 | < 0.70 | Missing wedge, over-regularization |
| `rsm_corr` high-arc bucket | ≥ 0.70 | 0.50–0.70 | < 0.50 | Cannot extrapolate into missing wedge |
| `ra_mae` | < 0.05 | 0.05–0.10 | > 0.10 | Anisotropy contrast lost or inflated |
| `fiber_symmetry_mae` | < 0.05 | 0.05–0.10 | > 0.10 | Angular artefacts from missing wedge |
| `orientation_error_mean` | < 5° | 5°–15° | > 15° | Orientation confused, smoothing too strong |
| `psnr` | > 25 dB | 20–25 dB | < 20 dB | Poor spatial fidelity of mean intensity |
| `ssim` | > 0.80 | 0.65–0.80 | < 0.65 | Spatial contrast or structure lost |
| `nrmse` | < 0.15 | 0.15–0.25 | > 0.25 | Mean intensity not well recovered |
| `holdout_nrmse` | < 0.10 | 0.10–0.20 | > 0.20 | Overfitting or over-smoothing |

A **working method** should satisfy all "good" thresholds simultaneously.  The most common
failure patterns are:

- **Missing-wedge artefacts**: high orientation error, large drop in `rsm_corr` for the
  high-arc bucket, elevated `fiber_symmetry_mae`.  Affects FBP most severely.
- **Over-regularization**: good SSIM, poor orientation error, compressed RA MAE (the method
  returns a smooth nearly-isotropic volume).  Triggered by excessive Laplacian weight.
- **Overfitting**: low training loss but high `holdout_nrmse`; RSM correlation may look
  artificially good if noise patterns are reproduced.
- **Normalization mismatch**: high RSM correlation but poor PSNR/NRMSE; the angular shape
  is correct but the absolute scale is off (common when the data container is not
  normalized consistently across mounts).
